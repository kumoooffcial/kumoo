Hollow Signal

A Short Story by kumoo

Relay Station RS-7 had missed its check-in for three days before anyone thought to send someone up the mountain about it. That was the job — Priya's job, specifically, field technician, third year, the kind of employee dependable enough to send somewhere without asking too many questions about why.

The access road was worse than the file suggested. By the time she reached the gate, the light was already failing, that thin mountain dusk that seems to fall sideways instead of down. The gate stood open. The generators were running — she could hear them, a low mechanical breath somewhere behind the building. The lights were on.

Nobody was there.

She found the maintenance log on the desk in the operations room, open to the last entry, dated six months ago. The handwriting grew smaller as it went, as if the person writing it had been trying to take up less space in the world.

Signal confirmed, it read. Do not tune below 87.4. Do not.

The name at the top of the log was Dr. Elara Voss. According to the file, she had been sent here alone to diagnose a fault in the relay equipment. According to the log, she had never left.

The radio receiver sat beside the log like it had been waiting for someone to pick it up. Priya turned the dial, more out of habit than curiosity, and a voice filled the small room — clean, warm, patient.

"Circuit breaker two is tripped. You'll want to reset it before you go any further. There's a flashlight in the second drawer."

There was. Exactly where the voice said it would be.

She told herself this made sense — automated diagnostic systems, pre-recorded guidance loops, nothing that couldn't be explained by a well-designed maintenance protocol. She told herself this for the next twenty minutes, right up until a second voice cut through the static, rougher, urgent, transmitting on a frequency the first voice had specifically told her was dead.

"She told you not to tune there," it said. "Why would you listen to something that already knows your name?"

Priya hadn't given her name to anyone. Not out loud. Not since she'd arrived.

She found Voss's personal journals scattered across the upper floors, one to a room, as if each entry had been written somewhere different because the previous room had stopped feeling safe. The early ones were clinical, professional — fault diagnosis, equipment notes, the ordinary boredom of remote work. By the fourth journal, the handwriting had started slanting, the sentences fragmenting.

The calm one knows things it shouldn't. I asked it a question I never said out loud, and it answered before I finished thinking it.

The other one is angry, but I think the anger is for me. I think it's trying to warn me.

I don't know which one is lying. I don't know if that's even the right question anymore.

By the time Priya found the sub-basement — a room with no schematic, no purpose, walls marked with something that might have been handwriting once, in a language that resolved into nonsense the longer she looked at it — the radio was speaking constantly, both voices now, overlapping, each insisting the other was the danger.

The calm voice: "You're so close. Just a little further. I only want to help you get home."

The distressed voice: "Don't listen. Don't trust the ones that sound kind. It learned kindness from someone. It learned it from her."

Priya stood in the sub-basement with her flashlight shaking in her hand and understood, with a clarity that felt less like realization and more like remembering something she'd always known, that Voss had stood in this exact spot, once, facing this exact choice, and that whatever answer she'd given had not been enough to bring her home.

The roof was the last climb. The antenna array stood black against a sky gone the color of an old bruise, and the radio in Priya's hand hummed with both voices at once, waiting for her to choose a frequency, to align the dish, to decide — with no way of confirming which voice had ever told her the truth — who to trust with the rest of her life.

She thought about the log entry that had started all of it. Do not tune below 87.4. Do not. Not a warning against a frequency. A warning against a choice already made, written by someone who had learned, too late, which voice she should have listened to.

Priya turned the dial past the frequency the calm voice had been guiding her toward all night — past the number that felt, in some indefinable way, too easy, too comfortable, too much like being told exactly what she wanted to hear — and found the rougher signal instead, the one that had done nothing but warn her, offer her nothing, promise her nothing except the truth as far as it understood it.

The antenna shuddered. Somewhere far below the mountain, a rescue frequency crackled to life, and a voice that was not calm, not warm, not designed to be trusted, said only: "Received. Hold position. We're coming to get you."

Weeks later, in a government office with fluorescent lighting and a form she had to sign three times, Priya was handed an audio file recovered from RS-7's archive — the final entry in Dr. Elara Voss's personal log, one she hadn't found in any of the six journals scattered through the station.

Voss's voice, recorded six months before Priya had ever driven up that mountain road, said only this: "I trusted the calm one. It was so easy to trust. It was perfect. It never once raised its voice at me."

A pause. Then, quieter: "I'm still here. I don't think I ever really left."

Priya deleted the file. She told herself it was protocol. She never went back up that mountain, and when her supervisor mentioned, almost as an aside, that a new technician had been dispatched to RS-7 for a routine equipment check, she did not ask when. She did not ask who.

She only hoped, in the quiet way people hope for things they can't quite justify, that whoever it was would know to trust the voice that never sounded kind.
