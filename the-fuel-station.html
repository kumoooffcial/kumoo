The Fuel Station

A Short Story by kumoo

The truck died the way old things die — not all at once, but in stages. First the headlights dimmed, then the engine coughed twice like it was clearing its throat, and then Rahul was sitting in the dark on National Highway 9 with nothing but the tick of a cooling engine and the sound of his own breathing.

Midnight. No signal on his phone. Fields on either side of the road, flat and black, stretching toward nothing.

He sat there for eleven minutes — he counted them, watching the clock on the dashboard because it was the only light left in the world — before he saw the sign, half a kilometer up the road, leaning at an angle that suggested no one had straightened it in a very long time. MISHRA FUEL & SERVICE. Beneath it, smaller, rusted almost to illegibility: Open 24 Hours.

Rahul almost laughed. Twenty-four hours. Sure.

But a walk was better than a night alone in a dead truck, so he took his flashlight, zipped his jacket against a cold that felt wrong for the season, and started walking.

The station emerged out of the dark like something surfacing from water. Three pumps, none of them lit. A convenience store with a shattered window taped over from the inside. A service bay with its door frozen half-open, like it had been interrupted mid-motion and simply given up.

There was no one there. Of course there was no one there. And yet the door to the store swung open at his touch, unlocked, as if it had been waiting.

Inside, dust lay over everything in a way that felt less like neglect and more like preservation — like a museum no one had thought to build. Shelves of expired snacks. A calendar on the wall, curled and yellow, still turned to a month that hadn't happened in over twenty-five years. A child's toy truck, abandoned on the counter, one wheel missing.

Rahul's flashlight beam caught something dark staining the floor near the register. He didn't look at it too long.

He found the first cassette tape in a drawer behind the counter, next to a dusty recorder that, impossibly, still had battery enough to play. He almost didn't press the button. He pressed it anyway.

"—testing, testing. If anyone finds this—" A woman's voice, young, frightened, trying to sound calm and failing. "My name is Sunita Mishra. My husband runs this station. If someone is listening to this, please, you have to know — it isn't safe here after dark. It was never safe. We just didn't want to believe it."

The tape hissed and clicked off.

Rahul stood very still in the dark store, flashlight trembling slightly in his hand, and understood — with the particular clarity that comes only in moments of genuine fear — that he was not alone in the building.

He heard it before he saw it. A dragging sound, slow, patient, coming from somewhere in the aisles behind him. Not footsteps exactly. Something that moved the way smoke moves, if smoke had weight and intention.

He didn't run. Running, some old animal instinct told him, was the wrong choice. He crouched instead, killed the flashlight, and let the dark swallow him whole. His heart beat so loud he was certain it could be heard. The dragging sound passed within feet of him — close enough that he felt the temperature drop, close enough that the shelf beside him rattled without being touched — and then it moved on, deeper into the store, and was gone.

Rahul did not move for a long time.

When he finally did, it was to search — quietly, carefully — for a way out that wasn't the way he'd come in. What he found instead, in the manager's office at the back of the store, was the rest of the story.

A police report, water-stained, dated 1998. Mishra family — reported missing — no bodies recovered — case closed, insufficient evidence. A stack of more cassette tapes, each one more frantic than the last. And a final entry, Sunita's voice barely a whisper by the end: "It doesn't want to hurt us. I don't think that's what it wants. I think it wants us to stay. I think it's been alone here for so long that it's forgotten the difference between company and captivity."

The tape ended mid-sentence.

Rahul thought about the toy truck on the counter, missing its wheel. He thought about a family who had simply stopped, one ordinary night, and never continued.

He thought about his own truck, dead on the highway, waiting for him.

The generator room was at the far end of the building, past the restrooms where he made the mistake of catching his reflection in a cracked mirror and seeing — for just a fraction of a second, gone before he could be sure he'd seen it at all — a second face standing behind his own. He didn't stop moving after that. He didn't look in mirrors again.

The generator took time to coax back to life — a fuse here, a canister of fuel there, his hands shaking through all of it — and the whole while, he could hear it. Circling. Patient. Getting closer with every minute the lights stayed off, as if his fear itself were something it could feed on, something that drew it in.

When the generator finally caught, the station flooded with a weak, flickering light, and for one moment Rahul saw it clearly — a shape in the doorway, tall and wrong, limbs bending at angles that didn't belong to anything that should walk upright. It recoiled from the light with something that might have been rage, or might have been a memory of a person, once, who had been afraid of the dark too.

Rahul didn't wait to find out which. He ran — past the pumps, past the sign that had brought him here, out onto the black ribbon of the highway, until his own truck rose up out of the dark like a promise. His hands were shaking too hard to find his keys at first. Behind him, he thought he heard something that might have been a voice, calling a name that wasn't his, into the dark it had always owned.

The truck started on the second try. He didn't remember the drive after that — only that when the sun came up, gray and ordinary over the fields, he pulled over and sat with his forehead against the wheel and cried the way he hadn't cried since he was a boy.

He never told anyone what happened at the Mishra station. Who would believe him? But sometimes, on long hauls, when the highway stretched dark and empty in front of him, he found himself glancing at the fuel gauge a little too often — not because he was worried about running out, but because he never, ever wanted to find out what waited for him if he did.
