Last Ward

A Short Story by kumoo

By the fourth night, Mira had stopped counting the dead by name and started counting them by floor.

Ashford Memorial had eleven floors before the outbreak. Now it had eleven separate arguments about which parts of the building could still be called safe. The ICU on the fifth floor had good sightlines and a door that actually locked. The basement had morphine, painkillers, and something that screamed in the dark whenever the generators cycled, which the three of them had agreed, without ever quite saying so, not to investigate.

"Radio tower's still the plan," Reuben said, for what felt like the hundredth time, hauling a crate of scavenged electronics up the stairwell. "Get it working, get a signal out, get rescued. Same plan as night one."

"Same plan as night one assumed we'd still be three people by night four," Selene said, not unkindly. She was checking the barricade on the ward door with the particular thoroughness of someone who had learned, badly, what happened when a barricade wasn't thorough enough.

They had started as five. Mira didn't let herself think about the other two more than she absolutely had to — not because she'd stopped caring, but because caring, she'd discovered, was a resource like any other, and she needed what little she had left of it to get through what remained of tonight.

Dawn was for scavenging — venturing into the quieter parts of the hospital, moving fast and light, gathering capacitors and wiring and whatever painkillers hadn't already been picked clean. Dusk was for fortification, nailing boards over windows that had already been boarded twice, checking locks that had already been checked. And night —

Night was for listening to the building fill up with things that used to be people.

The Walkers were the easy ones, slow and stupid, more obstacle than threat if you kept your head. The Sprinters were the reason nobody walked the east stairwell alone anymore. The Bloaters you smelled before you saw, and by the time you saw them it was already too late to do anything but run. And the Screamers —

The Screamers were the reason four people had died on night two, because sound was the one resource in Ashford Memorial that nobody could ration, and screaming brought everything in the building down on whoever made the mistake of being caught in the open when it happened.

"Seven nights," Mira said, mostly to herself, checking the component list against what they'd actually managed to scavenge. Six of the nine parts. Three more nights of searching, if they were lucky. Three more nights of not being lucky, if they weren't.

"You keep saying it like it's a countdown," Reuben said.

"It is a countdown."

"Countdowns end," Selene said, from the doorway, not looking at either of them. "That's the part I keep getting stuck on. What we're counting down to."

Nobody had a good answer for that, so nobody tried to give one.

The sixth night was the one that took Reuben. Not dramatically — there was no heroic last stand, no final words worth remembering. He went down to the basement for a part they needed and didn't come back up, and when Mira and Selene went looking, they found the door to the basement standing open in a way that told them everything they needed to know and nothing they wanted to.

They didn't speak much after that. There wasn't much left to say that wouldn't just be an admission that the plan — the whole fragile architecture of scavenging and fortifying and surviving until the radio tower could call for help — had always been a bet against odds that had never once favored them.

On the seventh night, Mira and Selene climbed to the roof with the last component clutched between them like something sacred, and the city spread out below Ashford Memorial in a way that made the whole outbreak look, absurdly, like it might be contained to just this one building, just this one impossible night.

The radio tower took eleven minutes to repair. It took considerably longer than that for either of them to work up the nerve to switch it on, because switching it on meant finding out, one way or another, whether the last seven nights had been worth what they'd cost.

Static, first. Then, faint but unmistakable, a voice — human, calm, professional in the specific way of someone reading from a script they'd read a hundred times already.

"This is Rescue Coordination. We have your signal. Hold position. Extraction team inbound, estimated arrival forty minutes."

Selene sat down on the rooftop concrete like her legs had simply stopped agreeing to hold her up. Mira stood very still, listening to the sound of a helicopter that didn't exist yet, somewhere out past the edge of what she could hear, and let herself, for the first time in seven nights, believe that it might actually come.

They didn't talk about Reuben while they waited. They didn't talk about the other two, either, or about the six days that had cost them everything they'd walked into Ashford Memorial with except each other. There would be time for that later, Mira told herself — on the helicopter, or after it, in whatever came next.

For now, there was only the rooftop, and the static, and the small, stubborn miracle of a voice that had finally answered back.
