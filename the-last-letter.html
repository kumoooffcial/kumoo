The Last Letter

A Short Story by kumoo

Margaret's hands trembled as she opened the mahogany desk that had belonged to her mother. Fifty years of dust settled on the polished surface, particles dancing in the afternoon light that streamed through the bedroom window. The smell of old wood and lavender sachets filled the room—a scent she hadn't encountered in decades, yet somehow remembered perfectly.

She had come to empty the house. The estate sale was scheduled for next week, and the lawyer's letter sat on the kitchen table downstairs, full of words about "final wishes" and "outstanding matters." But Margaret knew nothing about outstanding matters. Her mother had died three months ago at eighty-seven, after a brief illness. Everything had been settled. Everything had been said.

Or so she thought.

The third drawer from the top stuck slightly, and Margaret had to jiggle it with the same motion her mother once used—the exact same flick of the wrist that Margaret had watched a thousand times while doing her homework in this very room. Inside, beneath yellowed photographs and a bundle of old letters tied with ribbon, she found an envelope.

It was sealed with wax—actual wax, as if her mother had been writing in some distant past century rather than just three months ago. The envelope was addressed in her mother's handwriting: To Margaret. For when you're ready to know.

Margaret sat on the bed. The mattress still held the impression of where her mother had slept. She opened the envelope carefully, pulling out pages of dense, careful handwriting that filled three sheets entirely.

My dearest Margaret, the letter began. If you're reading this, I'm gone. And I imagine you're angry with me. You should be.

Margaret's eyes widened. She read on, her mother's voice becoming clearer with each sentence, as if the woman herself was sitting beside her, finally telling the truth.

The letter revealed that Margaret's father—the man she had called Dad for forty-three years—was not her biological father. Her real father was someone named James Chen, a man her mother had loved desperately before Margaret was born. They had been separated by circumstance, by family objections, by the simple bad luck of being born into a time and place where such unions were complicated. Margaret's mother had married her stepfather three months after discovering she was pregnant. He had been kind. He had been good. He had never known the truth.

But James had known. And he had written her mother letters for years. The bundle Margaret had seen in the drawer—those were from him. Her mother had kept every single one.

The letter continued: I've spent my entire life wondering if I made the right choice. I loved your father—I do, in a different way than I loved James. But every time I looked at you, I saw James's eyes. I saw his stubbornness. I saw his capacity for kindness. And I wondered what your life might have been if I had been brave enough to choose differently.

I'm telling you this not to hurt you, but to free you. I've included an address and a phone number. James is still alive—he's eighty-nine now, living in Vancouver with his wife and children. Our children, technically, though he never knew about you. I've left instructions with my lawyer to contact him, to tell him about you, only if you wish it. The choice is entirely yours. But I wanted you to know that you have another family, another possibility, another story waiting if you want it.

I also want you to know that the man who raised you loved you completely. He was your father in every way that mattered. But I would have regretted not giving you the option to know the rest of your story.

Margaret read the letter three times. She wept the first time. She was angry the second time. By the third reading, she found something like peace.

She picked up the bundle of letters from James, tied with faded blue ribbon. The postmarks spanned twenty years. She didn't open them—not yet. Those were her mother's private correspondence, a love story that had existed in the margins of her own life, unseen and unacknowledged.

Instead, she reached for her phone and found the lawyer's number. She called and asked about the instructions in the will. Yes, the lawyer confirmed, there was a sealed envelope with contact information for a James Chen in Vancouver. What did she wish to do?

Margaret looked around the bedroom. At the desk where her mother had written this confession. At the photographs on the dresser showing three generations of her family. At the late afternoon light turning everything golden.

"Send it," she said quietly. "Tell him about me. And... tell him my mother kept his letters."

After she hung up, Margaret sat in silence. Somewhere in Vancouver, a man she had never met would soon learn he had a daughter he never knew existed. Somewhere in the space between her two fathers—the one who raised her and the one she'd never met—Margaret discovered something unexpected.

She discovered that family was larger and more complicated than she had ever imagined. That love could take forms she had never considered. That the last letter her mother ever wrote had finally given her permission to write the next chapter of her own story.

And in that permission, she found something precious: not closure, but opening. Not an ending, but an invitation to know more of herself, to embrace more of her own history, to live a fuller and more honest life than she might have otherwise.

Margaret placed the letter back in its envelope and set it carefully on the nightstand. Tomorrow, she would pack it away with the bundle of letters tied in blue ribbon. Someday, she might read them. But tonight, she simply sat in the gathering darkness of her mother's bedroom, holding close the strange gift of a secret finally told.

Outside, the sun sank lower. The house settled around her with all its weight of history and memory and love. And Margaret—Margaret who had always thought her story was completely written—began to imagine what might come next.
