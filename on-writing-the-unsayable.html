On Writing the Unsayable

A Personal Essay by kumoo

There are things we cannot say. Things too large for our mouths, too complicated for our tongues, too heavy for the simple structures of grammar and vocabulary we've been given. We carry them inside ourselves like stones, smooth from worry, hot from the friction of being constantly re-examined and rejected.

I've spent my entire life trying to say these things.

Writing began for me not as art or ambition, but as a form of linguistic desperation. I was nine years old, and I couldn't tell my parents how afraid I was that their marriage was ending. So I wrote a story about a kingdom where the sun and moon refused to share the sky. I was thirteen, and I couldn't explain the strange, drowning feeling that came from realizing I wasn't who I thought I was. So I wrote about a girl who looked in a mirror and saw a stranger's face.

What I discovered was this: language is not a wall between us and the unsayable. It's a bridge. Not a perfect bridge—the planks are uneven, the railings are unreliable, and it never quite takes us to where we intended. But it's a bridge nonetheless.

The Space Between Words

The unsayable lives in the white space between words. It's the way a character's silence can tell us more than their dialogue. It's the sentence that trails off without a period, the paragraph break that holds an entire lifetime of meaning. It's what we don't write that becomes the most powerful part of what we do.

When I write about grief—and I have, many times—I'm never writing about grief itself. Grief is too large, too personal, too specific to the architecture of each individual heart. Instead, I write about the moment someone reaches for their dead loved one's coffee mug. I write about the way the light falls across an empty chair. I write about the sound of a door closing, and in that small, precise moment, readers who have grieved find their own loss reflected back to them.

The unsayable isn't found in big, grand statements. It's found in the tiny, true details that no one thinks to write down.

Permission Through Fiction

Perhaps the greatest gift of writing fiction is this: it gives us permission to explore thoughts and feelings we cannot explore in real life. A mother can write about hating motherhood without being a bad mother—the act of writing is the act of understanding. A man can write about his tenderness, his vulnerability, his capacity to break, without these things becoming who he is reduced to.

Writing the unsayable through fiction means we can hold contradictions. We can be strong and afraid simultaneously. We can love someone and be angry at them. We can believe in ourselves and doubt everything about ourselves in the same breath. Stories allow us the luxury of complexity that real life often doesn't permit.

And when someone reads these stories, they find themselves. Not in the plot or the characters' names, but in the emotional truth beneath the surface. They read a story about a woman learning to forgive herself, and they think of their own failures. They read about a man finding courage in an unexpected place, and they remember a moment when they were braver than they thought possible.

This is the miracle of writing: we take what is most intimately, painfully ours, and in doing so, we make it universal. We say the unsayable by not saying it directly. We communicate through metaphor and emotion and the careful arrangement of words that somehow, despite their imperfection, capture a sliver of truth.

The Healing Act

I've come to believe that writing is perhaps the most honest form of communication available to us. When we write, we cannot hide behind tone of voice or facial expressions. We cannot soften our words with a touch on the arm or a sympathetic smile. We must trust our words alone to carry the weight of what we're trying to say.

This is terrifying. This is also liberating.

When I write about my father's absence, I'm not performing his absence for an audience. I'm excavating it. I'm turning it over in my hands, examining every side of it, and in the process, I understand myself better. The story I write might not be the truth—truth in fiction is always a negotiation between what happened and what it felt like—but it's more truthful than a direct statement could ever be.

My therapist once told me that some things we can't talk about, we have to write about. Some emotions are too big for conversation, which demands back-and-forth, which demands explanation. Writing allows us to simply pour the thing out, uninterrupted, until it's exhausted itself on the page.

And then, slowly, we understand what we've been trying to say all along.

The Reader's Alchemy

But writing the unsayable is only half the equation. The other half belongs to the reader—the person who takes these words and transforms them into their own meaning, their own understanding, their own recognition of themselves.

A reader once told me they read one of my stories during the darkest period of their depression, and it helped them feel less alone. Another told me they finally understood their mother's perspective through a story I wrote from a mother's point of view. A third said they realized they'd been running from something in their life by watching a character in one of my stories face what they'd been avoiding.

This is the greatest gift of writing: it connects us across the silence. It says, in the clearest language available to us, that we are not alone in our unsayable feelings. That someone, somewhere, has felt the thing you cannot name. That your grief and your joy and your confusion and your fear have been witnessed, held, and transformed into words that might help you understand yourself better.

The Practice

Writing the unsayable is a practice, not a destination. I don't believe I'll ever fully say what needs to be said. The gulf between inner experience and external expression is simply too wide. But every time I sit down to write, I try to build a better bridge. I try to find words that are truer than the last time. I try to trust that the gap between what I mean and what I say matters less than the attempt to cross it.

And I keep writing because I have to. Because the alternative—keeping it all inside, where it hardens into bitterness or explodes into crisis—is no alternative at all.

Writing gives me the chance to take the unsayable and make it speakable, even if only through the language of story. It gives me the chance to turn my private anguish into something that might touch a reader and help them understand their own.

That is why I write. That is why I'll keep writing.

Because somewhere in the white space between my words, the unsayable lives. And sometimes, if I'm lucky and careful and brave enough, I can coax it out into the light.
